# Script to Shot List

Status: first lesson brief for discussion. The example is provisional; no app changes or generated assets are included.

## Place in the course

Start the movie-making workflow here, with a supplied script. The learner does not need to develop a story or choose generation tools first.

**Outcome:** turn a short script into a shot list that communicates the story and can be used to identify production assets next.

As a standalone resource, this lesson teaches the same process using any short script. The course assignment uses the example below or the learner's own 30-second script.

## Supplied script: Paper Boat

Exterior. A shallow puddle after rain.

A small paper boat floats toward a stone. Its pointed bow catches against the stone, and the boat stops. A hand reaches into the water and gently nudges it free. The boat drifts away across the open puddle.

No dialogue. One location, one recurring object, and a brief appearance by a hand. The story is a small journey, an obstacle, and a helping action.

## What the lesson teaches

Read the script for changes the audience needs to see: the boat is moving, it gets stuck, someone helps, and it moves again. Then choose a view that makes each change clear.

A shot is one continuous view of an action. A story beat may need more than one shot; a sentence is not automatically a shot. Start with the fewest shots that make the action understandable.

For each shot, record:

- **Purpose:** what the audience needs to understand or feel.
- **Framing:** what is visible and how close the camera is.
- **Action:** what changes during the shot, including its end state.
- **Approximate duration:** its share of the finished film, adjustable during editing.

## Worked shot list

| Shot | Purpose | Framing | Action and end state | Target duration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Establish the boat and its surroundings | Wide, low view of the puddle | The boat drifts left to right toward a visible stone | 5 seconds |
| 2 | Show the obstacle | Close view of the bow and stone | The bow touches the stone; the boat stops | 6 seconds |
| 3 | Make it clear the boat is stuck | Medium view holding the boat and stone in frame | Water ripples around the boat, but the bow remains caught | 5 seconds |
| 4 | Show the helping action | Close view with room for a hand to enter | A hand nudges the boat clear of the stone and withdraws | 8 seconds |
| 5 | Resolve the journey | Wider view of the open puddle | The freed boat drifts left to right, leaving the stone behind | 6 seconds |

Total target edit duration: **30 seconds**. These are planning estimates, not required generation lengths. Generated clips may need trimming, and timing should change if the edit feels slow or unclear.

## Explain the decisions

- The wide opening provides geography before cutting closer.
- The contact shot establishes why the boat stops; the held shot gives the audience time to register the problem.
- The helping action gets its own shot so the cause of the boat's release is visible.
- The final wider shot makes the change from trapped to free easy to see.
- Consistent travel direction and the same boat, stone, and lighting help the shots connect. We will specify the reference assets in the next lesson.

Five shots are a starting choice, not a rule. Shots 2 and 3 could become one shot if the contact and pause read clearly together. The hand interaction may require iteration during production; this is a planning example, not a claim that it has been generation-tested.

## Course assignment

Using the supplied script or your own short script:

1. Identify the essential story beats.
2. Plan three to five shots using the fields above.
3. Assign rough durations totaling about 30 seconds.
4. Read only the action column: does it still tell the whole story?

**Finished output:** a shot list ready for the next step, defining the assets each shot needs.

## Completion check

- Every shot has a clear storytelling purpose.
- The setup, obstacle, and resolution are visible without extra explanation.
- Actions and framing are specific enough to guide production.
- Adjacent shots connect without unexplained changes in position or action.
- Timing leaves room to understand the important moments.
